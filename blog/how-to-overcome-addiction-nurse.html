How to Overcome Addiction as a Nurse

By T. Lott, RN | Published 2026-05-19 | 5 min read

Help someone break free from addiction—from a 23-year nurse who's seen recovery work.

Twenty-three years in healthcare. I've seen addiction destroy careers, relationships, families. And I've seen recovery happen in the most unexpected ways.

The worst part? Nurses don't talk about it. We're healers. We're supposed to have it together. So we suffer in silence—popping pills, sipping during shifts, hiding hangovers behind dark sunglasses and caffeine. I know because I've sat with colleagues at 3 AM, listening to the breaking point conversations.

This isn't judgment. This is recognition. And recovery is possible.

Why Nurses Are Vulnerable to Addiction

Healthcare workers don't accidentally become addicted. The system sets us up.

Twelve-hour shifts. Chronic pain from physical labor. Watching patients die. Losing colleagues to burnout and suicide. The stress is relentless. And the solution? It's in the supply room.

We have access. We have stress. We have isolation (because admitting struggle feels like admitting failure). The combination is lethal.

Studies show healthcare workers abuse opioids at twice the rate of the general population. Alcohol dependency among nurses? Higher. Benzodiazepines? We know exactly how they work and how to hide the dependence.

I watched a brilliant ICU nurse—Sarah, 16 years experience—start with pain pills after back surgery. Three months later, she was diverting fentanyl from patients. Six months later, she was in a diversion program. Last I heard, she was two years sober and working as a case manager for addiction recovery.

Recovery works. But only if you start.

The First Step: Recognizing You Have a Problem

This is the hardest one.

Addiction in healthcare has a costume. It looks like ambition (working extra shifts to afford the drug), professionalism (never calling out, always showing up), and dedication (medicating the pain so you can keep helping others).

Here's what I see in colleagues with addiction:

  • Increasing isolation. Skipping social events. Avoiding people who knew you "before."
  • Defensive reactions when someone asks if they're okay. Anger that feels disproportionate.
  • Gaps in memory. Morning regret they can't quite articulate.
  • Neglecting responsibilities outside work (or inside work—calling out more frequently).
  • Physical changes: weight loss, poor hygiene, shakiness, blood pressure spikes.

The denial is strong. I've had colleagues tell me, "I only use on weekends," or "I can stop whenever I want"—while their hands shake and their families stage interventions.

One RN I knew told me, "I'm not an addict. Addicts are the people we take care of." Six months later, she was arrested for prescription fraud.

Recognition isn't weakness. It's survival.

Step Two: Find a Diversion Program or Treatment

This is where healthcare workers get lucky (and I mean lucky in a dark way).

Most states have nurse health programs (NPHPs) or diversion programs specifically for healthcare workers with substance use disorders. These programs are designed to:

  • Keep your license (if you're honest and compliant)
  • Provide treatment without public reporting
  • Monitor recovery with random drug screens
  • Rebuild your career while you heal

Why does this matter? Because jail doesn't fix addiction. Treatment does.

I know a surgeon who went through a diversion program. He was addicted to fentanyl. Five years later, he's back in the OR, sober, and now runs a peer support group for healthcare workers in recovery.

The hardest part is admitting you need help to your state board or employer. The fear is real. But the alternative—losing your license anyway, going to jail, dying—is worse.

Find your state's NPHP: https://www.addictioncenter.com/professional/nphp-nurses-state/

Step Three: Choose Recovery (Every Single Day)

Addiction treatment isn't a finish line. It's a daily choice.

I worked with a nurse named Marcus who was 18 months into sobriety. He told me, "Recovery isn't 'I quit drinking.' It's 'I'm actively choosing something better.'"

That stuck with me.

The practices that work:

  1. Attend meetings. AA, NA, SMART Recovery, peer groups for healthcare workers. The structure and accountability are non-negotiable.
  2. Find a sponsor or accountability partner. Someone who gets it. Someone who's been there.
  3. Therapy. Actual processing of the pain underneath the addiction. Most addicted nurses are self-medicating untreated trauma or mental illness.
  4. Change your environment. New friends, new habits, new routines. You can't stay sober in the same situations that got you high.
  5. Move your body. Exercise isn't optional. It rewires dopamine pathways and reduces cravings.
  6. Sleep. I know it's hard as a nurse. But sleep deprivation feeds cravings. Protect your sleep like your life depends on it.
  7. Help someone else. Sponsoring others, peer support, mentoring—giving back anchors recovery in meaning.

Recovery isn't about willpower. It's about connection, treatment, and building a life worth staying sober for.

What I've Learned About Healing

Twenty-three years of healthcare taught me: healing is possible. Even after you've hit bottom.

I've seen nurses go from diverted medication busts to running recovery programs. I've watched colleagues rebuild relationships with families they'd nearly destroyed. I've celebrated sobriety milestones and watched people find purpose again.

The ones who made it? They stopped hiding. They asked for help. They changed everything.

The ones who didn't? They died. Or they're still suffering.

If you're reading this and you know you have a problem, here's what I want you to know: You're not broken. Your brain is fighting chemistry and trauma. Treatment works. Recovery is hard. But it's possible.

You've already saved countless lives in your career. Now save your own.
