How To Overcome Addiction As A Nurse

By T. Lott, RN | Published 2026-05-19 | 5 min read

## The Reality That Changed Everything Help someone break free from addiction—from a 23-year nurse who's seen recovery work. When Marcus walked into my office—23 years into my nursing career—he was unrecognizable from the paramedic I'd worked with for eight years. Thin. Shaky. Eyes that had lost their light. "Ting," he said, "I need help. I can't do this alone anymore." That conversation changed how I approach everything. Not just his recovery, but mine. Not just his healing, but what it means to be someone in healthcare fighting their own battles. This isn't an article about addiction statistics or pharmaceutical interventions (though those matter). This is what I've learned sitting across from nurses, doctors, and first responders who looked exactly like Marcus—people who save lives while their own were falling apart. People who knew everything about clinical protocols for addiction and absolutely nothing about asking for help. ## The First Step Nobody Talks About In nursing school, they teach you to recognize addiction in patients. They don't teach you to recognize it in yourself. That's intentional, I think. There's a mythology in healthcare: we're healers. We're strong. We're trained to handle crisis. The idea that we could become addicted—that we could need help—contradicts everything we've been told about who we are. I watched Sarah, a brilliant surgical nurse I trained with, hide an opioid dependency for two years. Two years. She'd come to work exhausted, make devastating mistakes in charting, lose weight visibly—and nobody said anything. Not because we didn't care. We just didn't want to see it. The first step toward recovery, they always say, is admitting you have a problem. But the actual first step—the one they skip—is deciding that admitting the problem won't destroy you. For Marcus, that step took hitting bottom so hard that staying the same meant dying. For Sarah, it took getting caught. For me, it took watching people I loved choose recovery anyway. Here's what I know: You don't overcome addiction through willpower. Willpower is what got you through a 12-hour double shift running on inadequate staffing. Willpower is what made you skip lunch to finish charting. Willpower doesn't build new neural pathways. Willpower doesn't rebuild relationships. Willpower doesn't teach your brain how to feel pain without numbing it. ## What Actually Works (From Someone Who's Seen It) Over 23 years, I've watched three different recovery pathways transform people: **1. Clinical Programs That Actually Fit Schedules** This is where healthcare workers get stuck. Traditional inpatient programs assume you can just disappear for 28-90 days. As a nurse or doctor or paramedic, you can't. Your hospital needs you. Your family depends on your income. The guilt is already crushing. Some programs now offer intensive outpatient models specifically for healthcare workers. You go during hours that work for your job. You continue working (with adjusted schedules). You're in a group where everyone gets the absurdity of being a healthcare professional struggling with the very diseases you treat. Marcus did this. He worked 7 AM to 2 PM for three months, then went to a 4 PM support group where everyone else was also wearing scrubs and smelling like hand sanitizer. **2. Medication-Assisted Treatment (Without the Shame) I want to be direct: some forms of addiction recovery include medication. Buprenorphine. Naltrexone. These aren't "substituting" an addiction—they're treating a medical condition. Healthcare workers especially struggle with this. We know how opioids work. We understand dependency. We also carry this bizarre belief that "real" recovery means doing it without any chemical support. I've watched people fail five times on pure abstinence refuse one medication-assisted option and stabilize completely. I've also watched people destroy themselves refusing help that actually works because it didn't match their recovery fantasy. Get the help that works. Not the help that looks the most virtuous. **3. The Relationship Piece (Which Might Be The Whole Thing) Sarah told me something after six months sober: "The addiction wasn't about the drugs. The addiction was about being numb around people who were watching me disappoint them." Addiction in healthcare workers often sits inside a specific context: isolation, perfectionism, shame, systems that punish vulnerability. Recovery, then, isn't just about removing the substance. It's about coming back to people. Messy, vulnerable, unpolished people. Letting them see you struggle. This is the part they don't put in clinical programs. Marcus called me last month. He'd celebrated one year sober. Told me, "I'm still scared. I'm still an addict. But I'm not alone in it anymore." That's recovery. ## Resources Actually Worth Using If you're reading this and recognizing yourself—or someone else—here are the actual resources I tell people: **Recovery Dharma** (recoverydarma.org): Specifically designed for healthcare workers and people with trauma. Uses Buddhist psychology without requiring religious belief. **SAMHSA National Helpline**: 1-800-662-4357. Free, confidential, 24/7. They'll help you find healthcare-worker-friendly programs in your area. **CPNP (Collaborative Practice Network Program)**: Specifically monitors healthcare providers. It exists because your employer and regulators need to know you're serious about recovery—and this structure actually helps. **Medication-Assisted Treatment Locator**: SAMHSA's website has a searchable database. Search near you. Read the reviews from people in similar fields. **Peer Support Specifically for Healthcare Providers**: Look for "Caduceus groups" in your area. They're 12-step adjacent but specifically for doctors, nurses, pharmacists. You're in a room where everyone understands why you're afraid of losing your license. ## The Part They Don't Advertise Recovery is boring. After the crisis, after the intervention, after the treatment—you just... live. You go to work. You come home. You make dinner. You do your job. Nobody applauds. Nobody notices. It's not dramatic. That's why people quit. They expect recovery to feel good. It doesn't, at first. It feels like showing up. Day after day. To work. To appointments. To conversations you'd rather avoid. To people who watched you at your worst. Marcus sent me a text this morning. "Still sober. Still in the program. Still scared. But I'm doing it." That's the part worth talking about. ## A Message for Tomorrow If you're in healthcare and you're struggling—with substances, with alcohol, with prescription pills you "need" to manage your shifts—you need to know three things: 1. You are not special for being able to hide it. You're not strong. You're not in control. You're a human with a human problem in an inhuman system. 2. Recovery isn't choosing between your job and your life. It's choosing both. And yes, there are actually programs that let you do that. 3. The people watching you—the colleagues who notice, the family who's waiting—they don't need you to be perfect. They need you to be alive. That's it. That's the whole thing. ## One More Thing (Because I Wouldn't Be Ting if I Didn't Say This) Your healing matters. Not for your job. Not for your credentials. Not for anyone else's comfort. For you. You're allowed to want to feel better. You're allowed to ask for help. You're allowed to fail, try again, and fail again. You're allowed to be a nurse, a doctor, a paramedic who needed recovery—and still be all those things, still be good at those things. Ready to explore more about recovery and wellness?
