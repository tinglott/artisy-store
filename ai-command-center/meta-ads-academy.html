Measure honestly: attribution flatters, experiments tell the truth

Your Ads Manager dashboard will almost always make your ads look better than they are. Research assigned in MIT Sloan's marketing analytics course (Gordon and colleagues, comparing common measurement methods against big randomized experiments at Facebook) found that the usual approaches — comparing people who saw or clicked an ad with people who didn't — often fail to match what a true experiment shows. The reason is built into the machine: targeting deliberately shows your ad to the people already most likely to buy. Many of them would have bought anyway.

Lewis and Rao's study of 25 large advertising experiments adds the second hard truth: individual buying is so noisy that the true effect of a small campaign is usually smaller than the noise around it. A ROAS figure shown to two decimal places is precision theatre. At a small budget you usually cannot statistically prove that one campaign caused its sales — and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

So measure like a scientist on a budget:

  • Judge on outcomes, not applause. Purchases and qualified leads sent back through the Pixel and Conversions API — not clicks, likes, or reach.
  • Pool your evidence. One week proves little. Judge a creative family over a month of pooled results.
  • Use a holdout when you can. Once in a while, run Meta's lift/conversion-lift style test or a simple split (one region or audience held out). The honest comparison is people who would have been shown the ad but weren't — not "everyone else."
  • Bigger signals at small budgets. If purchases are rare, measure add-to-carts, checkout starts, or email signups — signals frequent enough to learn from.

Read the winner carefully

One more caution from recent research (Braun and Schwartz, on how platform A/B tools actually deliver): during a platform A/B test, the delivery system shows each variant to a different mix of people — the people it thinks each variant suits. So a platform "winner" means "this creative, delivered to the audience the algorithm chose for it, won." That's useful for picking what to run next. It is weaker evidence that the words themselves are better for everyone.

The house rule: before you rebuild all your copy around a winner, re-test it once in a fresh campaign with the same settings as the runner-up. If it wins again, it's real. Then shift budget the way adaptive-experiment research suggests: most of the budget to the leader, a small slice kept exploring new variants — learning while earning, instead of freezing on one ad until it wears out.

Before you spend — the 4-point check (from IBM's AI-marketing guidance)

  • AI or a template drafted it → you edited it into your own voice. (Research note: consumers can spot raw AI ads and often find them boring — add one real detail only you know.)
  • Every claim in it is something you can prove. No guaranteed results, no invented numbers.
  • It talks about the product — never "Are you…?" at the viewer (Meta's personal-attributes rule).
  • You know which single variable you'll test it against, and for how long.
