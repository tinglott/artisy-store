Why Nurses Burn Out — And What Finally Saved Me

By Ting Lott, RN • May 2026 • 6-minute read
After 23 years in emergency rooms and ICUs, I hit a wall that no nursing school, no supervisor, and no motivational poster ever prepared me for. I wasn't burned out from the work. I was burned out from invisible caring.

There's a phrase we use in healthcare: "compassion fatigue." It sounds clinical, manageable — like something you treat with a vacation or a bubble bath. But what I experienced was closer to a slow erasure of myself. I would walk into a 12-hour shift, give everything I had to every patient, every family member, every colleague — and walk out feeling like I had given away pieces of myself that weren't growing back.

The irony of being a nurse is this: you spend your entire career teaching people how to take care of themselves, while systematically ignoring every piece of advice you give them. Sleep? Optional. Meals? Eaten standing up. Stress management? Laughable when you're charting, answering call lights, and managing a full floor simultaneously.

"The people most likely to spot burnout in others are the last people to recognize it in themselves."

What Burnout Actually Looks Like From the Inside

Most people picture burnout as exhaustion. And yes, exhaustion is part of it. But the real signal — the one I wish someone had told me about — is emotional numbness. When you stop feeling sad when patients die. When you catch yourself thinking "just another admission" instead of seeing the terrified human in front of you. That's not being professional. That's your nervous system shutting down to protect itself.

For me, it started with irritability. Small things would set me off — things that never bothered me before. Then came the sleep problems (ironic for a night-shift nurse who should have been used to disrupted sleep). Then the physical symptoms: headaches, GI issues, constant low-grade infections because my immune system was too depleted to fight back.

The 3 Things That Actually Helped

1. Naming it out loud. The moment I said "I am burned out" to another person was the moment things began to shift. There is enormous power in not pretending. Healthcare culture rewards stoicism — don't let it steal your recovery from you.

2. Micro-boundaries, not macro ones. I couldn't quit. I had loans, a family, responsibilities. But I could stop answering work texts after midnight. I could take my full lunch break once a week. Small acts of self-preservation compound into something significant over time.

3. Returning to the reason. Somewhere under the paperwork and the politics and the 3 AM codes was the reason I became a nurse in the first place: I wanted to be there for people when no one else was. When I reconnected with that — through writing, through stories, through conversations with patients' families — something came back to life in me.

What Addiction Actually Looks Like From an ER Nurse's Eyes

By Ting Lott, RN • May 2026 • 7-minute read
I've watched addiction destroy marriages, careers, and families. I've also watched people — people everyone had written off — walk out of the darkest places imaginable and rebuild their entire lives. The difference was rarely what you'd expect.

In 23 years of emergency medicine, I have seen addiction up close more times than I can count. Opioids. Alcohol. Methamphetamine. Prescription painkillers that started with a legitimate surgery and spiraled into something that consumed everything. I have held the hands of people in withdrawal. I have called families to tell them their loved one didn't make it.

But I have also — and I need you to hold onto this — watched people come back. Completely. The homeless man who got clean and now runs a recovery nonprofit. The suburban mother whose children were taken away, who fought for years, got her family back, and now volunteers at treatment centers. The teenager who overdosed in my ER twice before finding what worked for him.

"Addiction is not a character flaw. It is a hijacked nervous system — and nervous systems can be rehabilitated."

What the ER Taught Me About Why People Use

The number one thing I want families to understand is this: nobody wakes up and chooses addiction. They choose relief. They choose escape from pain — physical, emotional, or both — that they don't know how to handle any other way. The substance becomes the solution before it becomes the problem.

I have watched brilliant, loving, capable people lose everything to substances — not because they were weak, but because they were in pain and found something that worked until it didn't. Understanding this is the first step to helping anyone in recovery. Including yourself.

The One Factor That Predicted Recovery More Than Anything Else

After 23 years, if I had to name one thing that separated the people who recovered from those who didn't, it would be this: they stopped being ashamed. Not all at once. Not easily. But at some point, they made peace with their story — not because it was okay, but because shame was the fuel that kept the addiction burning.

The moment someone could say "this happened to me, and I am more than this" without needing to hide from it — that was the turning point. Every time.

What You Can Do Right Now

If you are struggling with addiction, the most important thing you can do today is tell one person. Not to fix it. Not to get help yet. Just to say it out loud to someone who won't weaponize it against you. Secrets keep us sick. Truth starts the healing.

If you love someone in addiction: stop rescuing them from the consequences of using. Not with cruelty — with love. Every time you soften the landing, you delay their decision to seek help. This is the hardest thing a parent or spouse can do. It is also frequently the thing that saves them.

Suicide Doesn't Discriminate — 5 Things Every Family Should Know

By Ting Lott, RN • May 2026 • 8-minute read
The hardest conversations I have ever had in 23 years of nursing were with families who looked at me and said, "We had absolutely no idea." This article is for them. And for you — before you ever need to have that conversation.

If you are currently in crisis, please contact the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline by calling or texting 988. This article is for education and support — it is not a substitute for immediate help.

I have sat across from a lot of families in hospital waiting rooms and said things that changed their lives forever. Of everything I have witnessed in emergency medicine, suicide loss is the wound that never fully heals for the people left behind. The question I hear most — the one that haunts families for years — is "why didn't I see it?"

"The most dangerous assumption about suicide is that it only affects certain kinds of people. It doesn't. I have seen it touch every zip code, every profession, every income bracket."

1. The Warning Signs Are Rarely What You Expect

We are taught to look for sadness. But many people in suicidal crisis appear calm — even at peace — shortly before an attempt. This is sometimes because they have made a decision and the uncertainty is over. Watch for: giving away meaningful possessions, saying final goodbyes framed as "just in case," sudden calmness after a period of crisis, increased isolation, and statements that sound like permanent solutions ("everyone would be better off without me").

2. Asking Directly Does NOT Plant the Idea

This is one of the most persistent and dangerous myths in mental health. Research is consistent: asking someone directly if they are thinking about suicide does not increase risk. It frequently decreases it — because it breaks the isolation. Say the words: "Are you thinking about ending your life?" If they say yes, stay with them and call for help. If they say no, you have still opened a door they know they can walk through.

3. Means Restriction Saves Lives

If someone you love is in crisis, access to lethal means matters enormously. The time between the decision and the attempt is often very short. Remove firearms from the home temporarily. Secure medications. This is not about distrust — it is about creating time for the crisis to pass. Most people who survive attempts report that the urge was time-limited.

4. Recovery Is Real and Common

Over 90% of people who survive a suicide attempt do not go on to die by suicide. Many describe their recovery as the most transformational period of their lives — a time when they discovered what they actually needed that they weren't getting. Suicidal crisis is not a life sentence. With the right support, it is a pivot point.

5. You Cannot Love Someone Into Wanting to Live — But You Can Stay

Families often blame themselves: "I wasn't there enough." The truth is more complicated. You cannot manufacture someone else's will to live. But your consistent, non-judgmental presence — showing up without an agenda, without fixes, without platitudes — is one of the most powerful protective factors known to medicine. Just stay. Just be there. That matters more than you know.

5 Sleep Secrets From a Night-Shift RN Who's Tried Everything

By Ting Lott, RN • May 2026 • 5-minute read
I have worked night shift for more than a decade. If there is anyone on the planet who understands the particular misery of a shattered sleep schedule, it is a nurse who works 7 PM to 7 AM and then has to function as a normal human being during daylight hours.

The wellness industry sells sleep like it's simple: blackout curtains, lavender spray, white noise machine, done. What they don't tell you is what happens when your body's entire circadian rhythm has been systematically dismantled over years of shift work. These five strategies are what I actually use — not what looks good on a wellness Instagram account.

1. The 90-Minute Sleep Cycle Rule

Your body sleeps in 90-minute cycles. Waking in the middle of a cycle — whether naturally or by alarm — produces the groggy, disoriented feeling most shift workers know intimately. Calculate your wake time backwards in 90-minute increments from when your alarm goes off. If you need to wake at 6 PM, aim to fall asleep at 10:30 AM, 9 AM, or 7:30 AM. Waking at the end of a cycle rather than the middle changes everything.

2. Stop Fighting the Light — Work With It

Light is the most powerful regulator of your circadian rhythm. After a night shift, wear dark sunglasses on your drive home — not just darkened windows. This signals to your brain that night is continuing. When you're ready to wake up, open all curtains and get direct sunlight within 30 minutes of rising. Blue light therapy lamps ($30 on Amazon) also work for winter months or if sunlight isn't available.

3. The "Anchor Sleep" Strategy

If you can't sleep in one long block, use anchor sleep: two shorter blocks with a consistent "anchor" period that stays the same every day regardless of when your shifts are. For example, always sleep from 10 AM–2 PM, then add supplemental sleep as needed around it. The consistency trains your circadian rhythm even when your schedule is irregular.

4. Magnesium Glycinate Before Sleep

After years of experimenting with every sleep supplement on the market, the one I recommend most consistently to nursing colleagues is magnesium glycinate (not oxide, not citrate — glycinate specifically). 300-400mg taken 30-60 minutes before your sleep time supports GABA receptors, reduces cortisol, and promotes muscle relaxation without next-day grogginess. Talk to your provider before starting any supplement.

5. Create a "Transition Ritual" Between Work and Sleep

The shift-to-sleep transition is where most night-shift workers fail. You come home wired — adrenaline, caffeine, and 12 hours of sensory stimulation don't just switch off. You need a 20-30 minute decompression ritual: a specific playlist, a short walk, a hot shower, a cup of herbal tea. The content matters less than the consistency. Over time, your nervous system learns that this ritual means "we are done now." And it cooperates.

How Faith Carried Me Through My Darkest Hospital Shifts

By Ting Lott, RN • May 2026 • 6-minute read
I am not writing this to convert anyone or to sell you a religion. I am writing this because in 23 years of emergency medicine, I have seen the moments where medicine ran out — and I have seen what was still there when it did.

There is a specific quality of silence in a trauma bay after a team has done everything humanly possible and it still wasn't enough. Every nurse and doctor who has worked emergency medicine knows that silence. It is not peaceful. It is the sound of the limit of human knowledge and human effort.

I found faith — or perhaps faith found me — somewhere in those silences. Not a comfortable faith. Not a faith that explained why children get sick or why addicts die in their 20s or why the gentlest people sometimes suffer most. A faith that said: this suffering is real, and you are not alone in it, and there is something larger at work that does not require your understanding to be true.

"I have been in rooms where medicine had nothing left to offer. Those rooms taught me everything I know about faith."

The Patient Who Changed Everything

I will not use her real name. She was in her early 70s, brought in by EMS after a fall. Her prognosis was poor. Her family was on the way but hadn't arrived yet. I sat with her while we waited — hospital policy, nursing instinct, I'm not entirely sure which.

She wasn't scared. That was the thing. She looked at me with complete clarity and said, "Honey, I've been talking to Jesus for 72 years. He's not going to let me be afraid now." She died before her family arrived. But she was not alone, and she was not afraid. I have thought about her almost every week since.

Faith as a Clinical Tool

Research on spirituality and health is robust and often ignored by mainstream medicine: patients with strong spiritual practices have measurably better surgical outcomes, lower rates of depression, faster recovery times, and greater pain tolerance. This is not magic — it is likely the result of community, meaning, stress reduction, and the neurological effects of meditative prayer. But the outcomes are real.

As nurses, we are trained to assess spiritual needs as part of holistic care. In practice, this rarely happens. It should happen more. The question "what gives you strength right now?" takes 30 seconds to ask and often unlocks something in a patient that all the medication in the pharmacy cannot reach.

You Don't Have to Believe Everything to Believe in Something

I am a nurse and a scientist. I believe in evidence. I also believe in things I cannot fully explain — the way a dying patient's face changes in their final moments, the inexplicable sense of peace I have felt in hospital rooms at 3 AM, the way prayer — whatever form it takes — changes the person doing the praying.

If you are struggling, I am not here to hand you a religion. I am here to say: reaching for something larger than yourself, in whatever form that takes for you, has healed people I have watched medicine fail. That's not nothing. That might be everything.
