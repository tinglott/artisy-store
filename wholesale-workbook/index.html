Chapter 1

What Is Real Estate Wholesaling?

You find a great deal on a house, lock it up with a contract, then sell that contract to an investor for profit — without ever owning the house.

🧒 5th Grade Version

Imagine your friend wants to sell their bike for $50. You tell them "I'll buy it!" and get a signed note. Then you find someone who really wants that bike and sells it to them for $75. You never paid $50 — your investor paid $50, and you kept the $25 difference. That $25 is your assignment fee. That's wholesaling!

In real estate, instead of a $50 bike it's a $100,000 house. Instead of $25 profit it's $20,000–$30,000. And you never have to buy the house yourself.

💡
The 3 People In Every Deal

Seller — Wants to sell fast, often below market price (foreclosure, divorce, tired landlord) · You (Wholesaler) — Find the deal, lock it up, find a buyer · Investor/Buyer — Pays cash, fixes the house, makes their profit. Everyone wins.

Chapter 2

Exactly How You Get Paid

There are two ways to collect your money. Both are legal. The one you use depends on the type of deal.

📄

Method 1: Assignment

You sign a purchase contract with the seller, then "assign" (transfer) that contract to your investor for a fee. You never buy the house. Title goes directly from seller to investor.

Best for: Off-market deals where the seller agrees. Lower cost to you.

🔄

Method 2: Double Close

You briefly "buy" the house using short-term money (transactional funding), then immediately sell it to your investor at a higher price. Two closings happen back-to-back.

Best for: Auction.com deals where assignment is not allowed. Keeps your profit private.

💰 Real Deal Math Example

House After-Repair Value (ARV) $250,000
Investor's Rule: Pay no more than 70% of ARV $175,000
Minus estimated repairs − $30,000
Minus YOUR fee (assignment fee) − $25,000
Max you bid on Auction.com $120,000

Investor pays $145,000 → you keep $25,000 → investor fixes house → sells for $250,000 → investor profits $75,000. Everybody wins.

Chapter 3

Words You Need to Know

These are the real estate words you'll hear every day. Master them and you'll sound like a pro from day one.

ARV
After Repair Value. What the house will be worth AFTER an investor fixes it up. This is the most important number in every deal. Start here.
Assignment Fee
YOUR profit. The money an investor pays you to transfer your contract to them. Typically $5,000–$30,000+ per deal.
Purchase Contract
A legal paper that says "I agree to buy this house at this price." When you sign it, you control the deal. This is your #1 asset as a wholesaler.
Assignor
YOU. The person who found the deal, signed the original contract, and is selling their rights to an investor.
Assignee
Your INVESTOR/BUYER. The person who pays your fee and takes over your contract to actually close on the house.
Equitable Interest
A fancy legal term meaning you control the deal without owning the house. Once you sign a contract with the seller, you have equitable interest.
Double Close
When you actually buy the house and immediately sell it. Used when assignment isn't allowed (common on Auction.com). Requires short-term funding.
Transactional Funding
Short-term money (sometimes same-day) borrowed to fund the first purchase in a double close. Costs 1–2% of the purchase price. Paid back when your buyer closes.
MAO
Maximum Allowable Offer. The formula: ARV × 70% − Repairs − Your Fee = Highest you can bid. NEVER go over this number.
Cash Buyer
An investor who can buy the house without a mortgage. They close FAST (7–14 days). Building a list of cash buyers is your most valuable business asset.
Earnest Money
A small deposit (sometimes $100–$1,000) you put down when you sign the purchase contract. Shows the seller you're serious. You lose it if you walk away.
Title Company
A neutral business that handles the legal transfer of a house from seller to buyer. They hold the money and make sure the deal closes correctly.
REO
Real Estate Owned. A house the bank owns after foreclosure. Most Auction.com listings are REOs. Banks often prohibit assignment, so plan for a double close.
Buyer's Premium
An extra fee charged by Auction.com on top of your winning bid. Typically 5%. If you win a $100K bid, you pay $105K. Factor this into your MAO calculation.
Motivated Seller
A homeowner who NEEDS to sell quickly — often due to foreclosure, divorce, job loss, or being a tired landlord. They accept below-market prices. This is your target.

Chapter 4

The 8 Steps to Your First Deal

Click each step to expand the full instructions. Do these in order — every single time.

1

🏗️Set Up Your Business

Before you touch any deal — do this first

+

Think of this like building your tool belt before starting a job. You need the right foundation so you look professional and stay protected legally.

  • Form an LLC (Limited Liability Company)Go to your state's Secretary of State website. File for an LLC in your business name. Costs $50–$200. This protects your personal savings if anything goes wrong.
  • Open a Business Bank AccountTake your LLC documents to a bank. Open a separate account just for real estate money. Never mix personal and business money.
  • Hire a Real Estate AttorneyFind one who specializes in wholesaling or investor transactions. They'll review your contracts and keep you legal. Cost: $150–$300/hour. Worth every penny.
  • Get a Wholesaling-Friendly Purchase ContractYour attorney drafts or reviews a contract that includes assignment language. Key phrase to include: "[Buyer Name] and/or assigns." Those three words give you the right to assign.
  • Check Your State's Licensing LawsAs of 2025, 10 states require a real estate license after 1–2 assignments (PA, CT, MD, TN, OK, ND, and others). Know your state's rules BEFORE doing any deals.
⚠️
Don't Skip This Step

Many beginners jump straight to finding deals before setting up their business. Then they make money and don't know how to handle it legally. Set up first. Deals second.

2

👥Build Your Cash Buyer List

Find investors BEFORE you find deals — this is the secret

+

Most people think "find a deal first, then find a buyer." That's backwards. Build your buyer list first so when you get a deal, you can sell it in 24–72 hours.

  • Join Your Local REIA (Real Estate Investors Association)Search "[Your City] REIA" online. Attend monthly meetings. Introduce yourself as a wholesaler who brings deals. Collect business cards. These are your future buyers.
  • Search Facebook GroupsJoin groups like "Cash Buyers [Your City]," "[City] Real Estate Investors," and "We Buy Houses [City]." Post that you're a wholesaler with deals coming. Message active members.
  • Post on BiggerPockets.comCreate a free account. Post in your local forum. Say you're a new wholesaler looking to connect with cash buyers. Respond to every reply.
  • Call "We Buy Houses" SignsDrive around neighborhoods and call the phone numbers on those yellow signs. Those are investors. Tell them you're a wholesaler and ask if they want to be on your buyer list.
  • Build a Simple SpreadsheetName | Phone | Email | Markets They Buy In | Typical Budget | Preferred Property Type. Aim for 20–50 buyers before doing your first deal. 100+ is a power position.
💡
Pro Move: Reverse Wholesale

Ask buyers FIRST what they want (price range, neighborhoods, property type, condition). Then go find exactly that property. Your deal sells in hours, not weeks.

3

🔍Research Properties on Auction.com

Finding the right house at the right price

+

Auction.com is one of the largest online foreclosure and bank-owned property marketplaces. Here's how to use it to find deals.

  • Create a Free Account at Auction.comGo to Auction.com and register. It's free to browse. You only need to provide payment info when you're ready to bid. Explore the site and get comfortable with the filters.
  • Set Your Search FiltersFilter by: your target city/zip codes, property type (Single Family preferred for beginners), and price range below your MAO budget. Save your searches to get email alerts for new listings.
  • Study Each Listing CarefullyNote the starting bid, buyer's premium (usually 5%), estimated value, photos, and any property details. Check if there are liens, back taxes, or occupancy status. Read the "terms" section — this tells you if assignment is allowed.
  • Run Comparable Sales (Comps)Use Zillow, Redfin, or Propstream to find what similar houses nearby sold for in the last 90 days. This tells you the ARV. Compare: same neighborhood, similar size, similar condition after repairs.
  • Estimate RepairsUse the "$25 per square foot" rough rule for major rehabs, $10–15/sqft for light rehabs. Or use the app FlipperForce to estimate more accurately. Your investor will do their own inspection, but you need a ballpark first.
🏦
Important: Most Auction.com Deals = Double Close

Bank-owned (REO) properties on Auction.com usually have contracts that PROHIBIT assignment. Always read the purchase agreement before bidding. If it says "no assignment," you'll need to plan for a double close and have transactional funding ready.

4

🧮Run Your Numbers (The MAO Formula)

Never guess — always calculate before bidding

+

This formula protects you. If the math works, do the deal. If it doesn't, walk away — no matter how excited you feel.

🔢 The MAO Formula

Step 1: Find the ARV $250,000
Step 2: Multiply by 70% × 0.70 = $175,000
Step 3: Subtract repair costs − $30,000 = $145,000
Step 4: Subtract YOUR fee − $20,000 = $125,000
Step 5: Subtract Buyer's Premium (5%) ÷ 1.05 = ~$119,000
MAX BID on Auction.com $119,000
🚫
The #1 Mistake Beginners Make

Getting emotionally attached to a deal and bidding over MAO. If the math doesn't leave room for your fee AND the investor's profit AND repairs — it's not a deal. Move on. There will always be another property.

5

🔨Win the Bid & Get Under Contract

Placing your bid and locking up the deal

+
  • Register to Bid on Auction.comYou'll need to verify your identity and provide a credit card or proof of funds. Some auctions require a deposit just to bid. Know this before auction day.
  • Set Your Maximum Bid and Don't Change ItEnter your MAO as your maximum bid. Use Auction.com's auto-bid feature. Walk away if the price goes over your number. Discipline = profit.
  • Prepare Your Earnest Money DepositExpect 5–10% of the purchase price in certified funds or wire transfer. This is due immediately after winning. Have this ready BEFORE you bid. On a $120K win, that's $6,000–$12,000.
  • Read the Purchase AgreementWhen you win, Auction.com sends you a purchase contract. Read every line. Confirm: closing deadline (often 7–30 days), assignment clause status, fees, title issues, and property condition notes.
  • Do a Title Search ImmediatelyOrder a title search through a title company right after winning. Look for: unpaid liens, IRS tax liens, HOA dues, old mortgages. These can eat your profit or kill the deal. Find out NOW, not at closing.
⏰
Speed Is Everything

Closing timelines on Auction.com are tight — sometimes just 7 days. Have your transactional funder AND your investor lined up BEFORE you bid. Don't scramble after winning.

6

📢Market the Deal to Your Buyers

Selling the contract to your investor — fast

+

Once you're under contract, the clock is ticking. Your goal: find your buyer within 24–72 hours. This is why building your buyer list in Step 2 is so critical.

  • Send a "Deal Blast" to Your Buyer ListEmail/text every buyer on your list. Include: address, asking price (what you want them to pay), ARV, estimated repairs, photos, and close-by date. Keep it short and factual. Investors decide fast.
  • Share in Facebook Real Estate GroupsPost the deal (without your exact address until buyer is confirmed) in local investor groups. "Deal available in [zip code], ARV $250K, asking $145K, needs $30K in repairs. Cash buyers only, 14-day close." DMs only.
  • Contact Your Top 5 Buyers FirstCall — don't just text or email — your most reliable cash buyers first. Give them a 12-24 hour window to say yes before opening to others. Good deals go fast.
  • Be Honest About the PropertyDon't hide problems. If the roof needs replacing, say so. Your reputation is everything in this business. Investors who feel burned won't buy from you again — and they'll tell others.

📧 Sample Deal Blast Message

"🏠 DEAL ALERT — [City, State]
ARV: $250,000 (verified comps attached)
Asking: $145,000
Repairs: ~$30,000 (medium rehab)
Investor Profit Potential: ~$75,000
Close by: [Date — 14 days out]
Photos: [link]
Cash buyers only. No daisy chains. First verified buyer gets it. Reply or call [your number]."

7

✍️Sign the Assignment Contract

The legal paperwork that locks in your fee

+

Once your investor says yes, you sign a separate "Assignment of Contract" document. This transfers all your rights in the purchase agreement to them — and locks in your fee.

  • Use a Proper Assignment Contract TemplateYour real estate attorney should draft this. It must include: property address, original purchase price, your assignment fee amount, payment terms, and the effective date.
  • State Your Fee and Payment Terms ClearlyExample: "Assignee agrees to pay Assignor an assignment fee of $20,000. $3,000 due upon signing this agreement. $17,000 due at closing." The non-refundable deposit protects you if the buyer backs out.
  • Send to the Title CompanyYour title company handles the closing. Send them both documents: the original purchase contract AND the assignment contract. They'll confirm they can close the deal and coordinate both sides.
  • For Double Close: Arrange Transactional FundingIf doing a double close (no assignment allowed), contact a transactional funder. They wire money for Purchase 1 (you buying), you immediately close Purchase 2 (selling to investor), and their money is repaid same day.
📋
Key Contract Language

Assignment contract must say: "Assignor hereby assigns all rights, title, and interest in the Purchase Agreement dated [date] for property at [address] to Assignee." Without this exact transfer language, the contract may not hold up legally.

8

💵Close the Deal — Get Paid

The moment everything comes together

+
  • Attend or Monitor the ClosingYour title company coordinates everything. For an assignment, the investor closes with the original seller. You don't need to be there, but stay in contact with the title company to confirm everything is on track.
  • Receive Your Assignment Fee at ClosingThe title company writes you a check or wires your fee directly to your bank account at closing. This is YOUR payday. First check feels incredible — keep going.
  • Track Everything for TaxesAssignment fees are ordinary income. You'll pay self-employment taxes on them. Set aside 25–30% of every check for taxes. Work with a CPA who understands real estate investing.
  • Follow Up With Your BuyerAfter closing, text or call your investor. Ask how it went. Ask if they want more deals like this. A happy investor = repeat buyer = your business grows without spending more on marketing.
  • Document Your Deal for Future ReferenceSave the purchase contract, assignment contract, closing statement, and your profit calculation. Each completed deal teaches you something. Review what worked and what you'd do differently.
🎯
After Your First Deal: Scale It

One deal proves the model. Two deals builds confidence. Five deals builds a business. After deal #1, reinvest a portion into better tools (PropStream, DealMachine) and marketing to find more sellers and buyers. That's how $20K becomes $200K per year.

Real estate wholesaling is 100% legal when done correctly. The rules changed significantly in 2025. Know where your state stands before doing any deals.

🧒 Simple Version

"You can sell lemonade on your street, but you need to follow the city's health rules. Some cities make you get a permit. Same with wholesaling — legal everywhere, but some states have extra steps."

Legal Must-Haves

  • LLC formed in your state
  • Real estate attorney consulted about your state's rules
  • Purchase contract includes "and/or assigns" buyer language
  • Assignment contract template drafted by attorney
  • Seller is aware you may assign the contract (transparency)
  • Licensing requirement researched for your state

Chapter 9

The 7 Mistakes That Kill Deals

❌ Mistake 1: Bidding Without a Buyer Lined Up

You win the bid, then can't find an investor in time. You lose your earnest deposit and potentially get sued for breach of contract. Build your buyer list FIRST. Always.

❌ Mistake 2: Skipping the Title Search

Hidden IRS liens, old mortgages, or HOA judgments can appear at closing and wipe out your profit — or kill the deal entirely. Always order a title search immediately after winning.

❌ Mistake 3: Going Over Your MAO

Emotional bidding. The auction gets exciting, you go $10K over your number "just to win." Now there's no room for your fee. Stick to your formula. Walk away if needed.

❌ Mistake 4: Forgetting the Buyer's Premium

Auction.com charges 5% on top of the winning bid. If you forget this in your MAO calculation, your profit shrinks by thousands. Always divide your MAO by 1.05 when calculating your true maximum bid.

❌ Mistake 5: Assuming Assignment Is Allowed

Most Auction.com/REO deals prohibit assignment in the purchase contract. If you plan to assign without checking, you're in breach. Always read the contract. Plan for a double close as your default on Auction.com.

❌ Mistake 6: Not Setting Money Aside for Taxes

Assignment fees are taxable income. Many new wholesalers spend their first $20K check and get hit with a surprise tax bill later. Set aside 25–30% of every deal in a separate savings account from day one.

❌ Mistake 7: Not Being Transparent With Sellers

Hiding that you're a wholesaler or plan to assign their contract erodes trust and can cause legal problems. Always be clear: "I'm a real estate investor and may assign this contract to another buyer before closing." Transparency = protection.

Chapter 11

Questions Everyone Asks

Do I need money to start wholesaling? +
Less than you think — but not zero. You need: earnest money deposit (sometimes as little as $100–$1,000 for off-market deals, but 5–10% for Auction.com bids), attorney fees to set up contracts ($300–$1,000), LLC formation ($50–$200), and tool subscriptions. For Auction.com specifically, having $5,000–$15,000 available for deposits and earnest money is realistic. For purely off-market wholesaling, some people have started with under $1,000.
Is wholesaling legal everywhere? +
Yes — wholesaling is legal in all 50 states, but some states require disclosures or licenses. As of 2025, six states passed new regulations (PA, CT, MD, TN, OK, ND). Ten states require a real estate license after a set number of assignments per year. Always consult a local real estate attorney before your first deal. The legal landscape changes regularly.
Can I really assign an Auction.com contract? +
Usually no. Most Auction.com properties are bank-owned REOs, and the purchase contracts explicitly prohibit assignment. The standard approach for Auction.com is a double close — you briefly purchase the property (using transactional funding) and immediately resell to your investor. This is legal, widely used, and protects everyone in the deal. Always read the specific contract terms before bidding.
What if I can't find a buyer in time? +
This is why you build your buyer list BEFORE doing deals. If you're running out of time: call every buyer personally, lower your assignment fee to make the deal more attractive, post in every investor Facebook group in your area, and contact local hard money lenders (they know active flippers). If you truly can't find a buyer, you may lose your earnest deposit. This is why you NEVER bid without buyers ready.
How do I find transactional funding for a double close? +
Search "transactional funding [your state]" online, or ask your title company — they often have relationships with transactional funders. Also check BiggerPockets.com forums. Companies like Coastal Funding, Easy IBC, and similar specialize in this. It typically costs 1–2% of the loan amount and is repaid the same day when your investor closes. You need to show them proof that the end buyer has funds ready.
How much should my assignment fee be? +
There's no universal rule — your fee is whatever the deal math supports while still leaving enough profit for your investor. In lower-priced markets ($80K–$150K ARV), fees of $5,000–$10,000 are common. In mid-range markets ($200K–$350K ARV), $15,000–$30,000 is realistic. In hot markets or high-value properties, $30,000–$50,000+ is possible. The key: your investor needs to still make at minimum $30,000–$50,000+ in profit after buying from you or they won't come back.
Do I need a real estate license? +
In most states, no — wholesalers operate on their own behalf as principals (not agents representing others). However, some states now require a license after a certain number of assignments per year, and marketing yourself as a "real estate agent" without a license is illegal everywhere. If you plan to do 5+ deals per year, consult an attorney about your state's specific threshold. Having a license is never a bad thing — it gives you more tools and credibility.
How long until I close my first deal? +
Realistically: 30–90 days for your first deal if you focus consistently. Setup (LLC, attorney, contracts) takes 1–2 weeks. Building a buyer list takes 2–4 weeks of active networking. Finding and analyzing deals can take another 2–4 weeks. Once you have a deal and a buyer list, closing can happen in as little as 7–14 days. Some people close their first deal in 30 days. Others take 6 months. Consistency and action speed determine your timeline.
