How this course works

Every movement in this course follows the same six steps. Do not skip ahead — stance and weight distribution come before power.

1 · WatchStudy the diagram. See where the feet, hips, and hands go.
2 · IsolateSlow reps, no power. One movement at a time, in front of a mirror.
3 · CombineLayer it: add the movement to one you already know.
4 · ShadowPractice in rounds, moving and breathing, with no target.
5 · Bag / partnerONLY with a qualified coach, with supervision and proper gear.
6 · CheckUse the self-check at the end of each lesson. Film yourself and compare.

Teaching rules

  • Stance and weight before power. If the feet are wrong, the strike is wrong.
  • Slow to fast. Correct at half speed first. Speed comes last.
  • Mirror work. Watch your own feet, hips, and guard while you drill.
  • Video self-check. Film 30 seconds, watch it back, fix one thing.
  • Never full power without a coach. Power hides mistakes.
  • Spar only with supervision and proper gear. No exceptions.
  • Stop when hurt. Pain is a stop sign, not a test.

1 · The orthodox stance — top-down foot map

Lesson 1 · Sean Fagan's instruction for a right-handed (orthodox) practitioner.

Fig. 1 — Top-down map. Feet hip-distance apart. Right foot steps back by that same distance at 45°. Lead foot points at the opponent. Weight sits toward the back foot.

What to do: Spread your feet hip-distance apart. Step the right foot back by that same distance at a 45-degree angle. Point the lead foot directly at the opponent. Shift body weight toward the back foot rather than loading the front foot. Keep the stance slightly tilted — not bladed, not fully square. Hands high, palms facing outward or inward. Shoulders relaxed.

Thai swag: the continuous, relaxed, rhythmic weight shift between the front and rear feet. It keeps the shoulders loose and the body ready. A good stance makes two defenses easy: lifting the leg to check a kick, and parrying a push kick.

Self-check: ✓ Can you hold the stance and keep the Thai swag going for 60 seconds without the front foot taking the weight? ✓ Can you lift the lead leg into a check without stepping first?

2 · Rear body kick vs lead leg kick — side by side

Lessons 2 and 3 · Spring Sia — the same leg, two different jobs. The support leg tells you which is which.

Fig. 2 — Left: body kick rises up through the lower abdominals and hip flexors. Right: leg kick sinks low and chops down. The support leg is the giveaway.

Rear body kickLead leg kick
StepStep slightly across the center lineWide step across
Support legRise high onto the calf and ball of the foot; heel pivotsWeight dropped low; grounded, flat-footed
HipsDrive forward, extend through the targetSink down to build chopping momentum
ShinDrives through the ribs / midsectionChops downward into the opponent's thigh
FeelStretch up through lower abs and hip flexorsSink into the support leg

Self-check: ✓ Body kick — are you up on the ball of the support foot with the heel turned? ✓ Leg kick — is the support foot flat and your weight low? If the support leg is wrong, the kick is wrong.

3 · Wai Kru Ram Muay — the pre-fight ritual

Lesson 8 · Three parts, in order. Wai = pay respect · Kru = master or teacher · Ram = dance · Muay = boxing.

Fig. 3 — Circle the ring, kneel and bow three times, then dance. The Ram Muay is each gym's own movement — it is also a balance drill before competition.

Sarama — the music: a live band plays during the bout. The pi chawa (Thai oboe), drums, and cymbals set a rhythm that speeds up as the action speeds up. The music and the fight move together.

Teaching point: the ritual is not decoration. It teaches respect, it calms the fighter, and the dance itself is balance and footwork practice under pressure. Teach the meaning of each word before teaching the steps.

4 · Thai stadium pacing — rounds 1 to 5

Lesson 8 · Traditional bouts in Thailand do not start fast. The fight is built, round by round.

Fig. 4 — Slow start, fast finish. The first two rounds are a feeling-out process; the fight peaks in rounds 4 and 5.

Why rounds 1–2 are slow: fighters test each other with long jabs and light probes — defense, timing, and range. And the crowd is part of it: spectators and gamblers use these rounds to analyze the fighters and place live wagers. The pace accelerates in round 3 and peaks in rounds 4 and 5, when fighters engage in full offensive combinations.

Teaching point: patience is a skill. A student who rushes the first round has nothing left for the last one. Drill this: in shadow rounds 1 and 2, only jabs and probes; round 3 adds combinations; rounds 4 and 5 go full — and finish strong, because the finish is what the judges remember (see Diagram 9).

5 · The lead teep (push kick) — four steps

Lesson 2 · The teep sets distance, breaks rhythm, and opens combinations. Done right, the foot never breaks — the knee clears the elbows first.

Fig. 5 — Weight back, knee high, snap out, retract. The high knee is what keeps the foot safe: it clears the opponent's elbows so the foot does not fracture on contact.

Mechanics: shift weight onto the back leg. Pull the lead knee high toward the chest — this clears the opponent's elbows and prevents foot fractures. Snap the lead leg straight out and stab with the ball of the foot. The same-side arm swings down for leverage while the opposite hand stays glued to the face for protection. Retract cleanly back into position — do not fall forward and do not fall off balance backward.

Self-check: ✓ Does the teep retract without you falling forward? ✓ Is the opposite hand still on your face at contact? ✓ Did the knee come up before the foot went out?

6 · The rear elbow — step, palm, tuck, rotate

Lesson 5 · An elbow is a short weapon. The step makes the range; the tuck keeps you safe.

Fig. 6 — Step so the back foot follows and covers distance without overextending. Palm out for a pointier elbow. Opposite hand on the face, chin tucked. Rotate through — then stop.

Why the tuck matters: the chin tucked into the chest blocks incoming counter elbows. The hand glued to the face is the other half of that guard. An elbow thrown without the tuck is an invitation to be countered.

Self-check: ✓ After the strike, is your chin still tucked and your hand still on your face? ✓ Can you see the target the whole time — no back of the head showing?

7 · The liver shot — where it lands

Lesson 5 · Shane Campbell vs Remy Bonnel — the body shot that puts an opponent into "shutdown mode."

Fig. 7 — The liver sits at the 9th and 10th ribs on the right side. Digging knuckles in there impacts the vagus nerve and, in the guide's teaching, forces the body into shutdown mode.

The combination: Campbell used the liver shot alongside heavy low kicks, body shots, and Ernesto Hoost-style combinations to break down Bonnel. Concentrated power to the liver and waist breaks down an opponent's posture — and a broken posture cannot throw returning punches.

Teaching point: the liver shot is a lesson in targeting, not in power. Teach students to find the ribs first — in shadow, slow, with the hand — before any power is added. And like every strike in this course: bag and partner work only with a qualified coach, with supervision and proper gear.

8 · The pendulum leg kick — top-down footwork path

Lessons 2 and 4 · Spring Sia — the kick that curves around the counter. A straight line walks into a straight punch; a curve does not.

Fig. 8 — Rear foot steps forward to take the lead foot's place. The lead leg then swings outward in a subtle semicircle, so the shin reaches around and chops the opponent's back thigh.

Why the curve: the curved path keeps you from walking directly into the opponent's straight counter punches, and your posture stays correct on recovery. Teach it as footwork first — walk the path without kicking, ten slow reps — then add the shin.

Self-check: ✓ Does the arc stay curved — no straight line toward the opponent? ✓ Are you balanced when the foot lands back in stance?

9 · Thai stadium judging — whole fight vs round by round

Lesson 8 · Judges in Thailand score the fight as a whole. The finish matters more than the start.

Fig. 9 — Thai judging rewards the whole fight: composure, control, clean scoring strikes, and above all late-round dominance. A fighter moving backward while dictating the tempo can still be winning.

Teaching point: this is why pacing (Diagram 4) and the mind (Lesson 8) matter. Train students to finish every round strong — the last thing the judges see is the thing they remember. In practice rounds, score the final 30 seconds double, so students learn to close.

10 · The defense trio — check, clinch, switch kick

Lessons 1, 4, and 5 · Defense is not waiting. The check stops a kick, the clinch controls the fight, and the switch kick answers it.

Fig. 10 — The check: lift the leg 45°, top of the shin near the knee meets the elbow, blocking low or mid roundhouse kicks. The clinch: posture control, arms swimming inside, knees and elbows — while defending sweeps and turn dumps. The switch kick: a quick foot switch in place builds backward-to-forward momentum for a lead-leg body kick.

Jab — the fourth basic: a lead-hand straight punch. A slight lead-foot step, elbow pointed down until it rotates at extension, chin hidden behind the lead shoulder. Teach the jab first; it is the measuring tool for everything else.

Self-check: ✓ Check — does the shin meet the elbow at 45°, not the foot? ✓ Clinch — can you control posture without leaning on your partner? ✓ Switch kick — does the switch happen before the kick, in place?

11 · The combination layering workout — round by round

Lesson 6 · Spring Sia's home shadow boxing method. Start simple, add one thing per round. No equipment needed.

Fig. 11 — Start with teep and jab-cross. Each round adds one layer. By round 7 you are flowing through the full combination — and you built it one piece at a time.

Core circuit — finish every session with this

  • Forearm side planks
  • Sit-ups with alternating elbows
  • Shoulder taps
  • Leg raises with hip-ups
  • Russian twists

Do each for 30 seconds, rest 15, move to the next. Two rounds of the circuit. Breathe through every rep — never hold your breath.

Glossary — the words to know

TermMeaning

Switch kickLead-leg body kick after a rapid foot switch in place; backward-to-forward momentum powers the kick.

The 4-week home practice plan

No equipment needed. Three sessions a week, 20–30 minutes each. Shadow boxing only — bag and partner work need a qualified coach.

WeekFocusSessions (3× per week)
Week 1
Stance + teep		Lesson 1–2Stance and Thai swag: 60-second holds, mirror work. Teep steps 1–4, slow, 3 rounds of 2 minutes. Core circuit ×1.
Week 2
Kicks + check		Lesson 2–3Body kick vs leg kick: support-leg drill (rise vs sink), 10 slow reps each. Check at 45°, 3 rounds. Layering rounds 1–3. Core circuit ×1.
Week 3
Elbows + layering		Lesson 4–5Rear elbow: step, palm out, tuck, rotate — slow, no power. Pendulum footwork path: walk the curve 10 times. Layering rounds 1–5. Core circuit ×2.
Week 4
Full flow + mind		Lesson 6–8Full layering rounds 1–7 with active recovery between rounds. Practice pacing: rounds 1–2 slow, round 3 rising, rounds 4–5 strong finish. Wai Kru sequence, slow, as a warm-down. Core circuit ×2.

Rest days matter. Soreness is normal; sharp pain is a stop sign. If something hurts, stop and ask a qualified coach — do not push through it.

Assessment checkpoints — one per lesson

Can you do each of these? If not, stay on that lesson another week. There is no rush — turtle pace wins.

LessonCheckpoint — you can…
1 · FoundationsHold the stance with Thai swag for 60 seconds; lift into a check without stepping first.
2 · OpeningsTeep retracts without falling forward; pendulum arc stays curved, not straight.
3 · CombinationsFlow teep + jab-cross + check for a full 2-minute round without stopping.
4 · DeceptionSwitch kick: the foot switch happens in place before the kick; the feint reads as real.
5 · DefenseRear elbow: chin tucked, hand on face, no over-rotation; check meets the elbow at 45°.
6 · TrainingComplete layering rounds 1–7 with active recovery; finish the core circuit twice.
7 · LegendsName one thing each of the four legends does best — and show it in your own shadow round.
8 · MindShadow a full 5-round fight with the Thai pacing (slow 1–2, rising 3, peak 4–5) and finish strong.
